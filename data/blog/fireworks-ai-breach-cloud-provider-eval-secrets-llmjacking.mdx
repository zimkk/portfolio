# Fireworks AI Breach, Round Two: A Cloud Provider Hack Reached Dev Machines and Unfinished Model Weights

If you run anything on an AI inference platform, this one is worth ten minutes. Fireworks AI first told customers in early October that an attacker had used internal credentials to read environment variables from evaluation jobs. On November 9, 2026 it published an update saying there was a second, separate incident: the third party cloud provider hosting its R&D machines was compromised, and that opening led to Fireworks development machines. Coverage landed on November 10.

I want to be careful here, because most of the coverage I found is a rewrite of Fireworks' own statement. There is one primary source, a couple of outlets restating it, and a lot of open questions. I will mark what is confirmed and what is not.

## What Fireworks Has Actually Said

Pulling the two disclosures together, the timeline looks like this.

| Date (2026) | Event | Source status |
|---|---|---|
| October 6 | Fireworks spots misuse of internal credentials and starts containment | Stated by Fireworks |
| October 7 | Notifications to affected customers begin | Stated by Fireworks |
| October 9 to 10 | Employee API keys rotated, more user keys revoked, service account tokens rotated | Stated by Fireworks |
| October 23 | The cloud provider hosting Fireworks R&D machines has its own security incident | Stated by Fireworks |
| October 24 | An unauthorized party uses that access to reach development machines and run an unauthorized workload; Fireworks detects it the same day | Stated by Fireworks |
| October 25 | Fireworks says it sees no further successful access to the customer facing platform | Stated by Fireworks |
| November 9 | Public update with the wider impact | Stated by Fireworks |

The data Fireworks says was touched in the second incident falls into three buckets: resource metadata such as identifiers, names, descriptions and creation times; billing information including billing and shipping addresses, invoice and payment history, balance and credit usage; and a subset of incomplete model weights and datasets for some customers. Fireworks says card numbers were not exposed because it does not store them, and that its inference, Training SDK and Nexus products showed no detected impact.

Two things are not public. The cloud provider is not named, and the vulnerabilities involved have not been described. One outlet also noted that the disclosure does not establish theft of complete production models. That is an important detail, and also a reminder that "incomplete" is doing real work in that sentence.

## Why This Is Two Different Failures, Not One

It is tempting to file this as "Fireworks got hacked twice." I think that misses the lesson. The two incidents have different mechanisms.

The first was a secrets problem. Evaluation jobs received secrets through environment variables, an attacker with internal credentials could read them, and customer keys for other services were exposed. Fireworks itself reminded customers that deleting a stored secret does not revoke the underlying credential, which is a line every platform team should print and tape to a wall.

The second was a vendor trust problem. Your security posture includes the machines your vendor rents from someone else. When a hosting provider several layers down has an incident, the blast radius reaches your vendor's dev boxes, and from there it reaches your data. You never signed a contract with that provider and you never got to review its controls.

Fireworks also tied the earlier activity to a financially motivated group selling stolen access to LLMs, often called LLMjacking. That attribution is Fireworks' own and I have not seen independent confirmation. A Cloud Security Alliance research note from October 23 makes the point that no vendor has publicly tied the October platform incidents to one actor, and that the campaign framing came from a single company. So treat "one coordinated campaign" as a theory, not a fact.

## The Pattern Across October

This is not an isolated story. I covered the Railway incident, where customer environment variables may have been accessed, in my [Railway breach rotation playbook](/blogs/railway-breach-environment-variables-secrets-rotation-playbook). The CSA note groups Fireworks, Railway, OpenCode and a LiteLLM malicious package release into one cluster of platforms that hold other people's AI keys.

The shared target is clear even if the actor is not. Attackers are not attacking the model providers head on. They go after the intermediate platforms that store customer secrets, because a single stored OpenAI or Anthropic key is worth money the moment it works. Inference access is billable, resellable and hard to trace back.

## What Builders Should Do Now

Fireworks' own guidance is sensible, and I would add a few things from the security side.

1. If you got a notice, replace the revoked Fireworks keys and update your apps. Old keys will simply fail authentication, so plan a short window.
2. Rotate the third party credentials listed in your notice at the issuing provider, then update your secrets. Do this even if your logs look quiet.
3. Review logs on anything those credentials could reach, plus your own Fireworks account for unfamiliar users, API keys, training jobs, evaluators and datasets.
4. If you uploaded datasets or fine tuned weights, treat them as potentially exposed and decide whether that matters for you. Fireworks disabled self service dataset uploads and downloads for Batch Inference and dataset based training while it investigates, so expect friction.
5. If you did not get a notice, Fireworks says no incident specific action is required, but I would still rotate keys you gave it once, as hygiene.

Here is a quick script I would run to find out which of your repos and pipelines hold Fireworks keys at all. It only reads, nothing else.

```bash
# find likely Fireworks key usage across local repos (read only)
grep -rIn -e "FIREWORKS_API_KEY" -e "api.fireworks.ai" . | cut -c1-160
```

If a key shows up in a CI config, it belongs in your secret manager with a short lifetime, and the same goes for any long lived key sitting in a repo. The point is the same one I made in my guide on [multi tenant credential security](/blogs/multi-tenant-credential-security-n8n): one shared key across many workflows turns a small leak into a large one.

## The Lesson About Stored Secrets

Environment variables are a delivery mechanism, not a vault. Anything handed to a job at runtime can be read by something with enough access to that job. That is fine for a short lived, narrowly scoped token. It is a bad home for the master key of your whole AI bill.

My rules, which I would apply to any platform:

| Practice | Why it matters here |
|---|---|
| One key per platform and per environment | A leak at one vendor does not unlock the others |
| Provider side spend caps and usage alerts | LLMjacking shows up first as a billing anomaly |
| Short lived or scoped tokens where supported | Stolen keys expire before they are resold |
| Revoke at the issuer, never just delete the secret | Deletion hides the value, it does not kill it |
| Ask vendors where their dev and R&D boxes run | The second Fireworks incident came through a hosting layer |

The same logic applies to model serving infrastructure itself. When I wrote about the [Cloudflare Workers AI KV cache leak](/blogs/cloudflare-workers-ai-glm-5-3-kv-cache-leak-sglang-tenant-isolation), the problem was tenant isolation inside a shared serving stack. Different layer, same theme: shared infrastructure fails in ways the customer cannot see, so design as if it will.

## Third Party Trust Is Now an Engineering Problem

I keep seeing the same shape in incidents this autumn. A trusted dependency or vendor gets compromised, and the damage lands on teams that did nothing wrong. My write up on the [Polymarket third party script drainer](/blogs/polymarket-debugbear-third-party-script-wallet-drainer-supply-chain) was about a script running in the browser. This one is about a hosting provider under a vendor. The mechanism differs, but the conclusion is the same: you inherit the weakest link in a chain you cannot fully see.

That does not mean you should stop using vendors. It means you should know three things about every one that touches your keys or data. What secrets of yours does it hold, how fast can you rotate each one, and what is your ceiling for damage if that vendor is fully compromised tomorrow. If you cannot answer in a sentence each, that is your homework this week.

## My Take

Fireworks deserves some credit. It published a bulletin, named dates, listed data categories, told customers what to do and brought in outside incident responders. That is better than a lot of disclosures I have read. It also still has gaps: no cloud provider named, no description of how the internal credentials were first obtained, and an investigation that is still running. I would not call the story closed.

The honest read is that AI infrastructure companies are growing fast and accumulating customer secrets, datasets and weights faster than their security programs mature. Attackers have noticed. If your product depends on one of these platforms, your incident response plan needs a line item for "our vendor was breached", with a named owner and a rotation runbook you have actually tested.

Unverified for now: the claim that all the October incidents are one campaign, and any claim about the full extent of weights taken. I will update this post if Fireworks or the unnamed cloud provider publishes more.

## Key Takeaways

- Fireworks AI disclosed on **November 9, 2026** a second incident: a third party cloud provider hosting its R&D machines was compromised, leading to access to development machines on October 24.
- Exposed data includes resource metadata, billing details and **some incomplete model weights and datasets**. Card numbers were not stored, and inference products showed no detected impact.
- The cloud provider and the technical cause are **not disclosed**. Treat attribution to one campaign as a theory.
- **Rotate at the issuer**, not just in the vault. Deleting a stored secret does not revoke it.
- Use per platform keys, spend caps and short lived tokens so one vendor failure cannot become your whole bill.
