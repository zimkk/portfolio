# Microsoft 365 Outage: What Engineers Learn

Email going down used to be an annoying afternoon. In late 2026 it is a lot more than that, because email, calendar and files are the data layer that half your AI tooling reads from. On Thursday, November 12, 2026, Microsoft 365 started failing for users around the world, and by Friday morning Microsoft was still saying recovery was not going as planned. Outlook, Exchange Online, parts of Teams, Copilot, and the Microsoft 365 connectors that OpenAI and Anthropic offer all took damage.

I am writing this while the incident is still open, so treat the details below as a snapshot. I will mark what is confirmed, what differs between reports, and what nobody outside Microsoft can know yet.

## What Happened

The timeline, as reported by several outlets between November 12 and 13:

- Problems started on Thursday. Outlook outage reports spiked in the early afternoon Eastern time.
- Microsoft first pointed at elevated load on infrastructure, then described servers that were failing automatic health checks and no longer processing requests.
- Through Thursday night and into Friday, recovery stalled. Microsoft described its efforts as not progressing as expected and warned that restarted services could fall over again once traffic returned.
- By the Friday morning UTC update, Microsoft said a patch had improved stability on some servers and it was rolling the fix out more widely, with no restoration time given.

Microsoft also said, through an executive, that this was not a cybersecurity breach. Take that as the vendor's current position. It is plausible, and nothing I read contradicts it, but it is not something an outsider can verify.

| Claim | Figure | Who reported it |

The two peak numbers differ a little, which is normal when outlets sample a crowd sourced tracker at different minutes. Downdetector counts complaints, not affected users, so never read it as a headcount. The seat count is a ceiling on potential exposure, not a count of people who were down.

## What Microsoft Says Broke

This is the part worth reading slowly, because the public wording shifted.

Early coverage quoted Microsoft blaming elevated load. A later account said Microsoft clarified that this was not a general traffic surge. Its explanation was a drop in the number of servers available to handle load, together with a process that sets up secure connections becoming overwhelmed on the affected servers. Once servers failed health checks they were pulled out of rotation, which left even fewer servers to carry the same traffic.

If you have run production systems, you know that shape. It is a capacity cliff. I am inferring this from the public wording, not from a postmortem, and Microsoft has not published one yet. But the described behavior matches a familiar failure loop:

1. Some servers get slow or stop completing secure connection setup.
2. Health checks mark them unhealthy and remove them.
3. Remaining servers take more connections, so their handshake workload rises.
4. They fail health checks too, and the pool keeps shrinking.
5. Restarting a server does not help, because the moment it rejoins it is hit by the full queue of retrying clients.

That last step matches Microsoft's own warning that restarted services might become unresponsive again as soon as they received traffic. It also explains why the company talked about restoring traffic gradually and applying temporary traffic restrictions in some places. You cannot put a full load back on a cold pool. You have to ramp it.

Microsoft also reported testing a configuration change on a subset of servers before wider rollout, and reversing a recent network configuration change to see whether it had contributed. Whether that earlier change was the trigger is unknown. I would not draw conclusions from the fact that they checked.

## One Claim I Am Not Repeating as Fact

One report says Australia's Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister said Microsoft had compared the incident to the 2024 CrowdStrike event, which involved a faulty update. That is a secondhand statement of what a minister said Microsoft said. I did not find Microsoft stating it directly, so I am treating it as unverified. If it turns out to be right, it points at a bad change, not at plain demand. If it is wrong, it is a good reminder of how fast a framing sticks during an incident.

## The Blast Radius Is the Story

The scary part is not that Exchange Online had a bad day. Every large service does. It is how far the effects traveled.

Reported affected areas included:

- Exchange Online mailboxes, mail delivery and calendars.
- Teams calendar, meeting scheduling and chat creation for organizations using Information Barriers, while core chat and calls mostly worked.
- Copilot chat and its access to work data such as email, calendars and files.
- Security and compliance tooling including Defender and Purview.
- Planner, To Do, SharePoint Online, Power BI, Fabric, Graph, Windows 365, and some Intune functions.

Then there are third parties. OpenAI reported errors in the ChatGPT Microsoft 365 connector, and Anthropic said most email, calendar and search requests through the Claude Microsoft 365 connector failed. This is the dependency chain I keep warning teams about. You bought an AI assistant, the AI assistant reads your mail through a connector, and the connector is only as available as the mail service behind it. The assistant did not have an outage. Your data source did, and the assistant went down with it.

Real world damage was also reported. A US federal appeals court clerk's office was reported unable to send or receive email for much of Thursday. Japan's Finance Ministry reportedly could not receive email on Friday. Singapore's government technology agency listed government email among affected systems. A listed company reportedly postponed its quarterly results announcement. Service NSW in Australia told customers to use online channels. Some hospitals reported clerical problems while saying patient care was unaffected. I have only seen these through news coverage, so I cannot vouch for each one, but the pattern is consistent across outlets.

## The Sourcing Caveat

Almost everything above traces back to Microsoft's own service health updates and statements, relayed by outlets that did not have independent access to Microsoft's infrastructure. The Downdetector numbers and the third party connector notices are the only parts with a separate origin. So when two articles agree, that is not two independent confirmations of the root cause. It is one vendor statement, reported twice. Until Microsoft publishes a post incident review, the cause stays an account, not a finding.

## Why This Matters for Teams That Do Not Run Microsoft

It would be easy to read this and think it only hits Microsoft shops. It does not. Look at your own stack for the same pattern:

- Do you have one SaaS service that other services authenticate or read from?
- Do your AI agents, automations or workflows treat that service as always available?
- Do your health checks remove capacity faster than your system can recover it?
- Do your clients retry in a tight loop when they fail?

If you answered yes to any of those, you have a smaller version of this incident waiting for a bad day.

## What Engineers Should Do Now

Here is the practical list, ordered by how cheap it is to do.

First, have a communications fallback that does not depend on your primary mail. When email is the thing that is down, the status announcement cannot go out by email. Keep a second channel and a contact list that lives somewhere else. Test it once a quarter so you know the phone numbers still work.

Second, make your connectors fail loudly and gracefully. If an AI agent depends on a mail or calendar connector, it needs a timeout, a clear error message and a defined behavior for when the data source is missing. The worst outcome is an agent that quietly guesses without data and reports a confident answer. Treat a connector failure as a hard stop for any workflow that makes decisions from that data.

Third, audit your own health check logic. A health check that ejects a server after a short failure window can turn a partial problem into a full one. Rate limit how many nodes can be ejected at once. Many load balancers and orchestrators offer a maximum ejection percentage or a minimum healthy panic threshold. Use it. The goal is that a degraded pool still serves some traffic instead of removing itself entirely.

Fourth, add jitter and backoff to every client retry. If a million clients retry on the same schedule, you have built the second outage yourself. Exponential backoff with random jitter is old advice because it keeps working.

Fifth, practice cold start recovery. Can your service come back from zero with full demand waiting? Most teams test failover and never test a gradual traffic ramp. Make admission control and slow ramp a tested path, not something invented at 3 am.

Sixth, know your actual dependency map. Write down which internal workflows break if each major SaaS vendor is down for a day. You will find surprises. Compliance, audit trail and security tooling often sit on the same vendor as email, which means the tool you use to investigate an incident may be down during the incident.

Seventh, keep a degraded mode for people. Customers and staff need a way to reach you when the normal route fails. The Australian state service that pointed people to online channels is a good small example of telling users what still works.

## My Take

I do not think the lesson here is that Microsoft is unreliable. A service that large will have bad days, and publishing honest, frequent status updates during one is the right behavior. The criticism I would make is about our side of the contract. We have spent two years wiring AI assistants into mail, calendar and files, and almost nobody has written down what those assistants should do when the source is down. We built a new dependency on top of the oldest single point of failure in the office and called it productivity.

I also want to push back on the habit of waiting for a root cause and then treating it as a specific bug. In practice, large outages rarely hinge on one bad line. They hinge on a loop: something slows down, automation removes capacity, and recovery tooling cannot put it back safely. If Microsoft's public description is accurate, this one fits that mold. The fix for the vendor is better ejection limits and slower ramp. The fix for us is to assume the loop can happen to anything we run, and to design our own retries and health checks accordingly.

Finally, pay attention to the AI connector angle. It is the new part. A mail outage used to cost people time. A mail outage that takes down the AI layer on top of it costs automation, which means missed approvals, stalled agents and silent failures that nobody sees until Monday. If you run agents in production, this week is a good week to ask what they do when their data source returns errors.

## Key Takeaways

- Microsoft 365 services, including Exchange Online, Outlook, parts of Teams and Copilot, were disrupted starting November 12, 2026, with recovery still incomplete on the morning of November 13.
- Microsoft said servers failed health checks and stopped processing requests, tied to a secure connection process being overwhelmed, and said it was not a security breach. The public wording shifted from elevated load to fewer available servers, and no post incident review exists yet.
- Reported peak Outlook complaints on Downdetector were about 130,000 to 138,000, with impact visible in 74 countries. These are complaint counts, not user counts.
- OpenAI and Anthropic both reported problems with their Microsoft 365 connectors, which shows how an outage in a data source becomes an outage in the AI layer above it.
- The CrowdStrike comparison attributed to an Australian minister is secondhand and unverified.
- Engineers should limit health check ejection, add retry jitter, test gradual ramp recovery, give agents a hard stop on connector failure, and keep a communication channel that does not depend on primary email.
