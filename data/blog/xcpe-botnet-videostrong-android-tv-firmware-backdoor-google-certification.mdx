# Google Banned a Certified Android TV Maker After Its Firmware Turned 1.3 Million Boxes Into a Botnet

Most botnet stories start with a vulnerability. This one starts with a purchase order. Google's Threat Intelligence Group published a report on November 2, 2026 saying that Videostrong Technology, a Shenzhen based maker of Android TV boxes, built a backdoor into its own firmware, sold access to it, and passed Google's certification tests while doing so. Google calls the backdoor XCPE. It says about 1.3 million set-top boxes were enrolled, and that taking down the criminal affiliates cuts off access to at least 10 million more infected devices that came in through other routes.

A quick note on sourcing before I go further. The primary source is Google's own report, and I could not open it directly in this run, so everything below about it comes through three secondary write ups that quote or summarize it: Help Net Security, The Next Web and Malwarebytes. They agree with each other on the main points, but they are largely restating one statement from one company, plus some extra numbers from partner firms. I will flag the claims that rest on a single source.

## What Google Says Happened

The short version, as reported by all three outlets:

- Videostrong shipped Android TV boxes with a backdoor in the firmware. It runs with system privileges and reinstalls itself from a protected partition after a reboot, so uninstalling an app does not remove it.
- The backdoor can switch off Google Play Protect package verification and erase records of user consent, which lets other software install without the owner seeing a prompt.
- Videostrong did not just use it. Google says the company sold access to criminal affiliates, who loaded their own modules.
- The malicious code was activated after the first boot, which is how the devices cleared Google's compatibility and security tests. Google described this as a gap in its tests.
- Google banned Videostrong, plus any future subsidiaries and affiliates, from building certified Android devices or supplying software to certified vendors, and took legal action against control servers and domains. The Japan National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient and TrendAI helped.

Here is how the numbers line up across sources.

| Claim | Number | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| Boxes enrolled through the XCPE backdoor | About 1.3 million | Google |
| Additional devices cut off by disrupting affiliate infrastructure | At least 10 million | Google estimate |
| Infected devices counted between June and October | 1,472,554 across 206 models | TrendAI, reported by The Next Web |
| Model names Synthient published from the campaign | 182 | Synthient, reported by The Next Web |
| Typical count per country in the US, Germany, UK, Australia, Canada | Roughly 26,000 to 42,000 each | The Next Web |

The largest infection counts were reportedly in Malaysia, Brazil, Iraq, Mexico and Taiwan. Beyond TV boxes, Google also saw the backdoor on telehealth devices, karaoke machines and digital signage. One single source detail worth repeating carefully: Synthient linked the MECOOL brand to Videostrong, but it also cautioned that a model name alone does not prove a given unit is infected.

## What the Botnet Was Used For

Affiliates paid for access and ran modules. The reported uses were hidden ad fraud, enrolling boxes in residential proxy networks, stealing account details and system logs, mapping the home network, and installing more malware. Operators could also take live screenshots, inject keystrokes and open a remote shell.

The residential proxy angle deserves attention, because it is the one that quietly hurts people who never bought the box. A residential proxy sells the home IP address of an infected device. Traffic from criminals, including password guessing, then exits from a normal household connection and looks clean to IP reputation checks. Malwarebytes points out that the owner's address can end up associated with that abuse. If you run a login endpoint, some of the "residential" traffic you rate limit or challenge may be coming from devices like these.

Google also says that since August 2026 the operators started using droppers disguised as system packages. These reportedly use a privilege escalation exploit to write to the system partition, which makes cleanup even harder.

## Why Certification Did Not Save Anyone

This is the part I find most useful as an engineer. Videostrong was a certified Android partner. Its boxes passed the compatibility and security tests that buyers, retailers and even enterprises treat as a signal of trust. The Next Web adds that as of November 2 the company's own site still advertised products as Google certified.

The mechanism is not exotic. A test suite checks the device at a point in time, in a lab, usually shortly after a clean boot. If the bad behavior only starts later, the test never sees it. That is the same class of problem as any time based or condition based logic bomb, and it is the reason a passing test report is evidence of the tested behavior, not proof of the absence of the untested behavior.

Google says it is tightening firmware integrity checks for certified Android TV devices and changing its manufacturer review process. That is the right direction, but note what it implies. The fix is a better inspection of the supplier. It does not change the fact that a device maker has a root level position on your network, and a device maker's incentives are not yours.

I have been writing about the same shape all autumn. In my post on the [Polymarket third party script drainer](/blogs/polymarket-debugbear-third-party-script-wallet-drainer-supply-chain), a script from a vendor that sat inside a trusted page did the damage. In the write up on the [trojanized Claude Code extension on the Cursor marketplace](/blogs/trojanized-claude-code-extension-cursor-marketplace-ssh-backdoor), a marketplace listing was the delivery route. XCPE is the hardware version of that story: the dependency is the firmware, and the vendor is the attacker.

## Why the Takedown Does Not Clean the Boxes

Cutting off command servers stops the criminals from steering the botnet. It does not remove the code. Reports say the backdoor runs as a system app signed with the maker's key and restores itself from a protected partition, so a factory reset is not a reliable cure and ordinary owners cannot fix it. Malwarebytes says there is no dependable self check, and Google has not published a full list of affected models. The practical advice from that coverage is to disconnect a box you suspect, ask the seller or manufacturer for clean firmware or a replacement, and do not count on removing apps.

Unverified for now: I have not seen independent confirmation of the 10 million figure outside Google's own estimate, and I have not seen any vendor-neutral analysis of the firmware itself. If you read something that claims a definitive list of infected brands, be skeptical.

## What Engineers and Teams Should Do Now

You probably did not buy a TV box from a no name vendor for your office. But the lesson reaches further than TV boxes, because your network almost certainly has unmanaged devices on it: signage, conference room hardware, telehealth carts, kiosks, cheap streaming sticks that someone plugged into a meeting room display. Here is what I would do this week.

1. Inventory anything that runs Android and is not an enrolled managed device. Start from your DHCP and ARP data, not from asset spreadsheets, because the spreadsheet will miss the box someone brought in themselves.
2. Put those devices on an isolated network segment with no route to internal services and strict egress rules. The idea is the same one I laid out in my guide to [zero trust architecture implementation](/blogs/zero-trust-architecture-implementation): assume the device is hostile and limit what it can reach.
3. Watch egress from that segment. A signage player has no reason to talk to dozens of unknown hosts or to push high volumes of traffic out at night, which is what a residential proxy looks like.
4. For devices you can reach over ADB in a lab, do a read only check for the package names reported in public write ups. This is a weak heuristic, not a verdict, since Google has not published a clean indicator list.

```bash
# read only check on a lab device you own, connected over adb
adb shell pm list packages | grep -i -e oemservice -e xcpe
adb shell getprop ro.build.fingerprint
adb shell ls -l /oem 2>/dev/null
```

5. Buy hardware from vendors who publish firmware update policies and signed update chains, and treat a certification logo as a minimum bar rather than a guarantee.
6. If you operate login or signup endpoints, add detection that does not rely only on IP reputation. Device and behavior signals hold up better when attackers rent household addresses.

**Tip:** if a cheap box is already in your environment, do not wait for a perfect indicator. Isolating it costs a VLAN rule, while guessing wrong costs a foothold inside your network.

## The Same Lesson Industrial Teams Already Learned

If this sounds like operational technology, it is. In my breakdown of the [French supermarket freezer attacks on Danfoss controllers](/blogs/french-supermarket-fridge-hack-danfoss-controllers-ot-exposure), the core problem was a boring device nobody monitored, reachable from places it should not be. A TV box and a refrigeration controller are very different machines, but they share the same weakness: they sit on your network, nobody patches them on schedule, and their owners treat them as appliances instead of computers.

The supply chain angle also rhymes with the cloud story from earlier this week. When I wrote about the [Fireworks AI breach through a cloud provider](/blogs/fireworks-ai-breach-cloud-provider-eval-secrets-llmjacking), the lesson was that your posture includes vendors you never contracted with. Here it is the same idea at the hardware layer. You trusted the retailer, the retailer trusted the certification, and the certification trusted a lab test.

## My Take

Google deserves credit for doing something unusual: it named a certified partner, banned it, and admitted a gap in its own tests. A lot of platform companies would have quietly delisted a vendor and said nothing. The takedown with law enforcement and security firms is real work.

But I would not call this solved. The infected boxes are still in living rooms, hotels and offices, and the people who own them mostly have no way to know. The cleanup depends on a manufacturer that just got banned, which is not a group likely to ship a helpful firmware update. The honest outcome for many of these devices is that they should be unplugged and replaced.

The bigger point for builders is that certification is a signal about the vendor at one moment, not a property of the running device. If you build anything that treats a device as trusted because of where it came from, such as a signage fleet, an embedded client or a hospital cart, you need controls that assume the firmware might lie to you. Segment it, watch its traffic, and make sure the worst case is a bad afternoon and not a breach.

## Key Takeaways

- Google's Threat Intelligence Group said on **November 2, 2026** that Videostrong Technology built the XCPE firmware backdoor and sold access to criminal affiliates.
- About **1.3 million** boxes were enrolled, and Google estimates at least 10 million more devices lost their criminal controllers. Partner counts differ and come from other firms.
- The backdoor persists in firmware, disables Play Protect verification and survives app removal, so **the takedown does not clean the devices**.
- The maker passed certification because the malicious code activated after first boot, which shows the limits of point in time testing.
- Treat unmanaged Android devices as hostile: inventory them, isolate them and watch their egress.
- Single source caveat: most coverage restates Google's own report, so numbers beyond it deserve a careful read.
